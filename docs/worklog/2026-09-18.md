## #1089 — the day's real find, and the day's real mistake

**A day the household did $3.50 worse than doing nothing published as EPR 242.7%.**

```
EPR = (j_ref - j_ach) / (j_ref - j_star)

j_ref   5.4897      numerator    j_ref - j_ach  =  -3.4975
j_ach   8.9872      denominator  j_ref - j_star =  -1.4411
j_star  6.9308      EPR = +2.427   ->   published 242.7%
```

**Both terms are negative and the signs cancel.** A negative numerator is honest —
EPR goes negative and the dashboard says so. A negative denominator inverts it,
which makes this **the only failure mode in this scorer that makes the headline read
better than the truth.** The same report published `theoretical_maximum_yield:
-1.4411` — "the best available outcome was worse than nothing" — and nobody had
noticed.

Nothing caught it because **neither existing reliability signal involves `j_ref`**:
`soc_discrepancy_reliable` (#533) tests the SoC reconstruction, `regret_reliable`
(#956) tests `j_ach - j_star`. `regret_dollars` was **+2.0564** — positive — so
`regret_reliable` was True and `epr_reason` was None. Regret ≥ 0 asks "did the oracle
beat the achieved dispatch?", never "did the oracle beat *idle*?".

The day was flagged at all only by an **unrelated** SoC disagreement. **With a clean
reconstruction, 242.7% would have published as fully `epr_reliable`.** It escaped by
coincidence, not design.

Two of the three days in that report's own history carry structurally impossible
arithmetic by two different routes — 15 Sep with `regret_dollars` **-0.3977**, 17 Sep
with the negative denominator. Only 16 Sep, the day the P2P-gating fix was verified
on, is sound.

### The mistake

I wrote the fix on the claim that `j_star <= j_ref` holds **by construction** — the
oracle may always choose to do nothing, so it cannot be beaten by doing nothing —
making any violation proof of a computation fault, specifically #1081's pricing-path
mismatch.

**That claim is wrong, and this repo had measured why on 2026-09-16.**
`_widen_export_pin_to_achieved()`'s own docstring, written for #1001:

> Then the commitment is a **loss the oracle cannot decline** ... a 12 kW commitment
> over seven hours at 7.5c export against 37c import gives **`j_star` $2.43 worse
> than doing nothing at all** and regret -$4.95.

`fixed_export_kw` pins the oracle's export in every committed P2P period, so idle is
**not in its feasible set** and it can be beaten by idle with no pricing mismatch at
all. I presented as a new structural impossibility something already written down
here — **the same error corrected on #999 the day before.** That is twice in two
days, and the reusable lesson is not "read more carefully" but *check whether the
codebase already answers the question before deciding the answer is new.*

The 17 Sep report carries evidence of both mechanisms against its -1.4411
denominator:

```
p2p_commitment_shortfall_kwh   2.0036     -> #1001, a committed export pin was active
j_star_path_delta              2.4889     -> #1081, LP objective vs evaluator pricing
```

Either alone accounts for the sign and the report cannot apportion between them.

### What the correction improved

It made the reason string's wording load-bearing rather than incidental.
`oracle_not_better_than_idle` names the **observation** and no cause — which matters
because under #1001 a household hitting this has **nothing to fix in the code**, so a
reason blaming a pricing bug would be wrong roughly half the time. There is now a
test that fails if anyone renames it to something diagnostic.

The guard is right under either mechanism, for different reasons: under #1001 the
arithmetic is sound and the **interpretation** breaks (EPR measures capture against an
idle baseline the oracle was never free to choose); under #1081 the two inputs are
non-comparable. Both times the published percentage is not a score, and both times
the remedy is a decision rather than a patch — so it reports and leaves the number
alone.

### Two smaller things from the same change

- **`solver/epr.py`'s docstring was part of the defect.** It named exactly one
  mechanism for `epr > 1` — "j_ach beat j_star" — so a reader diagnosing 242.7%
  follows that advice, checks regret, finds +2.06, and concludes the number is sound.
- **A bare `j_star > j_ref` test fires on float noise.** `compute_epr()` has always
  treated `abs(j_ref - j_star) < 1e-9` as "no opportunity existed" and reported 1.0
  inside it, so a -1e-12 denominator on a genuinely flat day would have been reported
  as a perfect 1.0 **and** as unreliable simultaneously. The band is now a shared
  constant so the two cannot disagree about the same day — reuse, not a new
  threshold.

The check lives in `solver/epr.py` and is computed inside `compute_epr()`, so it
rides on `EPRResult` and every consumer gets it for free. That is what lets the
**standalone/cron writer** publish it: that deployment emits `epr` and
`theoretical_maximum_yield` and has never carried *any* of the three reliability
signals. Putting the check next to the arithmetic makes it free there rather than a
second copy that can drift (#357). The other two remain absent — a #944-family
structural gap, tracked rather than quietly closed.

## #937 — a prediction of its own, tested

#937's body explicitly rules out comparing its day-ahead figures to the one-step
`load_nowcast_skill_*` fields, and *predicts* those will "look considerably better
without the forecaster having improved one bit." Reading the issue first is what
stopped a false comparison being posted; the prediction itself was then testable
against four full days:

```
mean nowcast value-add      +$0.097 / day   (+$0.161 excluding a 0.958-coverage day)
#937's day-ahead figure     -$0.71  / day
```

Prediction holds. The part that was not predicted: **the magnitudes are not
comparable either, and not in the forecaster's favour.** Nowcast wins are small
(best day +$0.45, three of four under ±$0.10) while day-ahead losses average ~7×
larger and reach -$3.42. A forecaster that adds cents where decisions are cheap and
costs dollars where they are expensive is still costing dollars — and day-ahead is
the horizon that drives dispatch. That sharpens #937 rather than softening it, and is
consistent with the horizon-mismatch mechanism already established there.

### #773 — the production path was reporting a solvable model as infeasible

The pinned-LP tolerance work started as a `LexOptions`-only curiosity and turned into
the day's most consequential fix. `_lp_tolerance_matching_mip()`'s docstring claimed
`phase2_pin_resolve` was *"the single place asking an LP to re-certify a point a MIP
already accepted"*. There were **five**, and one was guarded.

`phase3_lex_restore` reproduced deterministically on unmodified code — the first
end-to-end reproduction #773 has had that does not require disabling a fix first,
which is the gap #999 filed. Then, following the mechanism into the mode production
actually runs:

```
CalibratedOptions →  Plan.status "infeasible", total_cost None
LexOptions        →  Plan.status "optimal",    cost 24.4997
```

Same model. `_calibrate_blend_weight()` ran its probes on pinned binaries through raw
`h.run()`, and `_primary_acceptable()` treats a non-optimal status as *"this weight is
unacceptable"* — so a probe landing in the band never raised, logged no diagnostic,
and **never reached `network.py`'s fallback**. Dispatch went unavailable with the
documented safety net not firing, because nothing raised.

Two things worth keeping. The mechanism was established by **elimination** — widening
phase 3's secondary-bound epsilon one hundred fold left the failure bit-identical,
which killed the competing #981-class explanation. And wrapping only the probe was
measured and found **insufficient**: non-optimal runs went 3 → 1 and the status stayed
`infeasible` until the final blended solve was wrapped too.

Mark independently re-verified this by reverting to `129a500` and confirming the test
genuinely fails without it. **What it does not fix** is pinned by its own test: a
violation of **1.92e-06**, above `mip_feasibility_tolerance` itself, which matching
tolerances cannot reach by construction.

### #1109 and #1111 — the same drift, twice, found by asking rather than noticing

Checking whether #768's stated prerequisites had actually been satisfied found that
one had only half carried. `_resolve_battery_participant_history()` — by its own
docstring *"the history-based sibling of `build_extra_batteries()`"* — set
`unavailable_period_indices` but not `shared_charger_group`/`shared_charger_max_kw`.
The oracle could charge two EVs on one 25 kW charger simultaneously.

Rather than stop there, diffing the two siblings' `BatteryConfig` keyword sets — a
five-line AST script — found **four** differences. Two were legitimate and documented.
The other two were `must_have_soc_by_period_index`/`must_have_soc_kwh`: a **hard**

oracle leave the car empty at departure and bank the arbitrage.

Both are now fixed, both **widened** rather than passed straight through — adding a
constraint narrows the oracle, and #956 records what that costs when the achieved
trajectory falls outside it. For the departure deadline the failure is *ordinary*: a
household that simply did not plug in has a day that misses its own target.

The durable half is a **parity test** asserting the only remaining differences are the
three with written reasons. The check that found both is now automated, so a third
instance fails rather than waiting to be noticed.

**#937 (v0.94.391)** — measuring #937's own horizon diagnostic across all eighteen
circuits produced the day's last real finding, and it reframes the issue.

**Nine of eighteen circuits publish an empty `validation_recursive_mae`** and therefore
select on one-step error rather than the 4-hour recursive metric. The gate is
`_recursive_multistep_mae()` counting an error only for a genuinely OBSERVED forward
point — observation **density**, not history length. Training volume does not separate
the groups at all: the empty side holds most of the largest sets, and the two smallest
circuits land on opposite sides (521 empty, 505 populated).

That makes it **structural rather than a warm-up**. A circuit whose sensor idles and
reports on change has a sparse validation window forever; those loads select on
one-step error permanently. And they are disproportionately the **deferrable** ones —
hot water, oven, heater — exactly the loads a dispatch optimiser most wants a forward
view of.

The fallback log said *"Too few origins"* for both causes, which sent a reader looking
for more history that cannot help. Now it names which fired. Log message only; the
selection rule is untouched with four tests pinning that, because changing which model
is chosen is #937's own open decision.

Where the metric does exist, naive persistence beats both models outright on two
circuits (both pool pumps, by 2.4x and 3.1x), is within 5% on two more, and is
genuinely beaten on five — a more textured picture than "the forecaster loses", and it
points somewhere: the models win where there is real structure, naive wins on periodic
pump loads.

- **v0.94.388** reports an away participant as `participant_away_during_window` instead

### The experiment, and sixteen occurrences of a bug that was never there

Devhub was **not touched** during the NUC1 upgrade — no deploy, no restart.
Within about a minute, every canonical reading on devhub flipped together:
`sensor.nimbus_solver_battery_forecast`, `..._quality_report`,
`sensor.nimbus_offer_curve` and `sensor.nimbus_household_load_total_forecast`
all moved 0.94.375 → 0.94.391 at the same `last_updated`, and the plan sensor
briefly went to `unknown` with 6 attributes — an entity coming up from a restart,
which NUC1 had just done and devhub had not.

Intervention on one side only, so this is causal. **The canonical
`sensor.nimbus_*` ids on devhub carry NUC1's install.** Every "devhub is running
stale code" conclusion in the sixteen recorded occurrences of that bug was
reading NUC1's release lag off devhub's screen.

The rule that failed is worth more than the finding. `ha_get_entity(<id>).platform`
returns `platform: nimbus_load` with devhub's own config-entry id for these
entities, and on 2026-09-17 that was used to overturn the correct belief and
conclude the canonical entities were devhub's own and genuinely stale.
**Registry ownership is not authorship** — the registry records which integration
claimed an entity_id; anything reaching `hass.states.async_set` or
`POST /api/states/` can write the state, and `platform` cannot see it. The
replacement check is *does an entity that only the new release creates exist* — a
foreign writer can post to an entity_id but cannot register one.

Two side mysteries fall out of the same mechanism: the duplicate-unique-ID
refusals at boot, and the recorder's oversize-attribute warnings firing every
~15 s on exactly those ids (a foreign write carries no `state_info`, so
`_unrecorded_attributes` never applies — the #944 path). Neither needs its own
explanation. A third was checked and dismissed before it could become an issue:
the stock `cast` integration logged the *same* duplicate-unique-ID error for 17
media players in the same boot, which makes that class environmental rather than
a nimbus defect — and `sensor.async_setup_entry` was read first to confirm it
builds and adds its batch once, with a scripted cross-check finding zero
duplicate `(entity_id_prefix, entity_id_suffix)` pairs across all seven
`FLATTENED_ATTRS*` tables.

### #773: the warm-start proposal fails its own correctness bar

#999's open lead was to hand phase 1's solution to phase 2 as a MIP start. Two
prior measurements had no power because every synthetic phase 2 resolved at the
root. **That premise is wrong** — synthetic phase 2 does branch, up to 372 nodes
/ 144k iterations, and the branching points sit at *smaller* period counts with
more loads, the opposite of where the search had been looking. Two points also
reproduce the production `Time limit reached` failure end to end.

With real power, the warm start is not safe: on one scenario it more than doubled
the cost (113 → 1449 nodes), and on two others it **changed phase 2's answer** —
0.16037 vs 0.17845, and 1.18853 vs 1.16646 — deterministically, three repeats
each, byte-identical.

Forcing `mip_rel_gap` to 0 does not reconcile those: both runs then report
`gap = 0.0` with `mip_dual_bound` equal to the primal, i.e. both claim a proof,
while disagreeing by up to 11 %. Same model, same phase 1, same tie row. The
working hypothesis — that a near-equality bound ~1e-7 wide across a ~12k-term
float sum is numerically ill-posed, making #773's `Infeasible`, its timeouts and
this wrong-but-confident optimum one phenomenon — is **explicitly not verified**
and was deliberately not posted as a finding. Only the narrow, settled part went
public: the warm start changes the answer, so it should not ship.

## Evening: v0.94.393, and three of this session's own #773 framings retracted by its own follow-ups

### #937 got a measurement, and it reframes the decision

v0.94.391 made the recursive-validation fallback name *which* of its two causes fired. It never said whether the fallback's answer is any **different**. Half this fleet uses that fallback permanently, so the question is the whole issue.

It is directly checkable on the circuits carrying both metrics — `argmin(validation_mae)` against `argmin(validation_recursive_mae)`:

```
reference household                        dev install
pw_pool1   naive  naive  YES               lt_l1      gbrt  gbrt  YES
pw_pool_2  naive  naive  YES               pw_ac_l2   gbrt  knn   NO
pw_comms   gbrt   knn    NO                pw_comms   knn   knn   YES
pw_ldry    gbrt   gbrt   YES               pw_l1      knn   knn   YES
pw_l1      gbrt   knn    NO                pw_l2      gbrt  gbrt  YES
lt_l1      gbrt   gbrt   YES
pw_l2      knn    knn    YES               4 of 5
pw_ac_l2   gbrt   gbrt   YES
pw_ac_b1   gbrt   gbrt   YES

7 of 9                                     11 of 14 combined
```

**All three disagreements, across two independent installs, are one-step preferring `gbrt` where recursive prefers `knn`. None goes the other way.** And the *circuits* that disagree differ between installs — `pw_comms`/`pw_l1` miss on one and agree on the other — so it is not a property of particular loads. What survives is the direction.

That direction is predicted by this package's own docstring, from the 2026-08-17 root-cause of Grid's GBRT blow-up: k-NN's prediction is a weighted average of observed `y_train` values, a convex combination and therefore bounded; GBRT is an unbounded additive sum with no clipping. Recursive validation is the only one of the two criteria that feeds predictions back as inputs, so it is the only one that can see the difference.

**So the fallback does not fail randomly — it systematically over-picks the model class most exposed to recursive drift.** That turns #937's open decision from "half the fleet may have the wrong model" into "half the fleet may be over-selecting GBRT, and on that half we cannot tell which", which is smaller, better posed, and has cheap candidate mitigations that need no recursive data at all.

**v0.94.393** makes the comparison a standing log line rather than a hand-run query, so the agreement *rate* accumulates instead of being one snapshot. Logged rather than published deliberately: it is evidence for a decision not yet taken, and publishing it would invite something to depend on it first. Selection itself is unchanged, pinned by an AST walk.

### #773: three framings retracted, and what survived

The morning's warm-start experiment produced a headline — "the production path returns a solution 1.89% worse while reporting Optimal with an invalid dual bound" — that did not survive its own follow-ups. Recording the sequence because the *pattern* is the lesson, not any individual result.

**Retraction 1.** The detection compared a cold run against a warm-started one, so the warm start was the unusual element. Removing it entirely — dump the phase-2 model, reload in a fresh HiGHS instance at zero gap, solve from scratch — **five of six scenarios agree with the in-process run**, including the headline one. "The proof is invalid" is unsupported.

**Retraction 2.** The remaining outlier, 16x24 s2, was described as "the production answer fails scrutiny". Measuring the live solver state at the moment phase 2 returns shows its accepted point sits *exactly* on the tie-row bound with `max_primal_infeasibility = 1.717e-07` — **above `primal_feasibility_tolerance` (1e-7) and below `mip_feasibility_tolerance` (1e-6)**, in the gap between the two that #979 already had to close elsewhere. Nothing fails; the point is feasible to the MIP and not to a tightened LP, which is exactly why pinning it returns `Solve error`.

**Retraction 3**, from the morning: the warm start as a candidate fix. It changes phase 2's answer in both directions on two of six scenarios. That verdict stands.

**What survived** is one description covering all of it: the tie row is binding at the optimum, the achievable secondary is extremely sensitive to exactly where on that boundary the point lands, and the boundary is defined at 1e-7 — the same order as the tolerances used to decide whether a point is on it. `_LEX_PRIMARY_TIE_ABS_SLACK` is set to the LP's primal tolerance while phase 2 is a MIP judged at ten times that.

The discriminating experiment — do three independent methods **agree** at a given slack, which is not confounded by feasible-set size the way comparing objective *levels* is — gives 2 of 4 at 1e-7 and **3 of 4 at 1e-6**, fixing the headline scenario. Partial confirmation with a live residual, and explicitly **not** grounds for changing the constant: improving agreement on four synthetic instances does not justify touching something that guards a real primary-cost guarantee on a live-dispatch install.
